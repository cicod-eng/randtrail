Crypto Account Security in South Africa

Last updated: 14 September 2026

For South African crypto users, account security starts with an uncomfortable fact: a strong exchange password is not enough. If an attacker takes over your mobile number or the email account that controls your password resets, they can work around protections that looked secure on paper. SIM-swap fraud remains a significant problem in South Africa, and SMS one-time PINs are especially exposed because they follow your mobile number rather than your physical device. The practical defence is layered: secure the email account that resets everything else, move important accounts off SMS-based authentication where possible, use unique passwords or passkeys, switch on withdrawal restrictions on your exchange, and harden your mobile account against unauthorised SIM changes. This guide walks through that setup in the order that matters.

It is educational information, not security or financial advice. No setup makes an account unhackable — the goal is to make the common, high-volume attacks stop working on you.

Quick answer

  • Any account that can be reset by SMS is a liability. Fix the email first — it is the master key — then the exchange, then the phone number itself.
  • Replace SMS 2FA with an authenticator app (Google Authenticator or Binance Authenticator), then switch SMS off. Save the backup key on paper, offline.
  • On Binance, turn on the anti-phishing code, withdrawal address whitelist and passkeys. OKX offers the same kinds of settings in its security centre.
  • Lock the number itself: ask your network what SIM-swap protection is available (Vodacom has a two-hour swap window you can stop on 082 135), and register a free SAFPS Protective Registration.
  • Keep only short-term trading money on an exchange and move long-term holdings to self-custody.
  • If you are being swapped right now: call your network to reverse it, tell your exchange to freeze withdrawals, retake your email, change passwords, and report to SAPS and SAFPS.

Why SIM-swap risk deserves special attention in South Africa

SIM-swap fraud is not a theory here; it is a measurable, persistent problem. South Africa’s banking risk centre SABRIC reported that SIM swaps formed part of the modus operandi in 7,657 mobile-banking fraud incidents in 2022, representing 76% of reported incidents in that channel. Those are banking figures, not crypto figures, but the mechanism transfers directly: an SMS one-time PIN follows the SIM, not your physical phone, so once a number is moved, the attacker receives your codes without ever touching your device. A 2026 BioCatch survey found 75% of South African bank fraud leaders reported increasing fraud attempts at their institutions, and local outlets flagged a spike in SIM-swap incidents within a two-week stretch in July 2026.

Two features make crypto accounts an especially attractive target for this kind of takeover. First, an on-chain transfer, once made, is generally irreversible: there is no fraud desk to reverse it the way a bank can claw back a card payment. Second, many crypto exchange account-recovery flows involve email or mobile verification — two layers that can become vulnerable when a phone number or email account is compromised. That combination is why the defensive order below starts with SMS.

Step 1: Replace SMS 2FA with an authenticator app

An authenticator app generates codes on your device, not through your phone number, so a stolen number gets the attacker nothing. Do this on your primary email first, then on every exchange and wallet account.

  1. Download an authenticator — Google Authenticator or Binance Authenticator from the official app store. Google Authenticator can run in “use without an account” mode, which keeps the codes only on the device; it also offers encrypted account sync, which is more convenient for recovery but makes the security of that Google Account more important. If you keep the codes device-only, the trade-off is recovery — losing the phone without a backup key makes restoring access harder.
  2. Log in to your exchange or email and open the security settings, then look for Two-Factor Authentication → Authenticator App.
  3. Scan the QR code shown on screen (or type the setup key manually), then enter the six-digit code the app produces to confirm.
  4. Write down the backup key — the 16-character setup key or recovery code — and store it offline, on paper. If you lose your phone without it, account recovery can take weeks.
  5. Go back and switch off SMS 2FA. Leaving it on keeps the back door open even after the authenticator is working.

One thing to understand about the order: if you secure the exchange but leave your email on SMS, an attacker can reset the exchange login through your email. The email is the master key, which is the next step.

Step 2: Lock down the email that resets everything

Your email can reset almost every other account you own, so it deserves better protection than the exchange itself.

  • Put an authenticator app on the email account, exactly as in Step 1.
  • Avoid relying on your phone number as the only recovery method. If your email provider allows stronger alternatives such as an authenticator, passkey or recovery codes, prefer those over SMS-based recovery. Review secondary recovery emails as well, and make sure any account capable of recovering your primary email has equally strong security.
  • Use a dedicated email address for crypto that you do not use for shopping, social media or mailing lists. Fewer places holding that address means fewer breach lists it can appear on.
  • Check forwarding rules and logged-in devices. After a takeover, attackers often add a forwarding rule so they keep reading your mail even after you change the password. Delete anything you did not set up yourself.

Step 3: Passwords — unique, and managed

The old advice — “use a strong password” — is only half the problem. Reused passwords are how one leaked site becomes access to your exchange. Use a password manager to generate and store a different random password for every account, and add an authenticator app to the password manager itself, since it now holds everything. A memorable passphrase you type nowhere else is a workable alternative for the few accounts you must remember.

Step 4: Turn on your exchange’s withdrawal locks

This is where a compromised login stops being a total loss. Binance gives you three controls that matter most, all in the account’s security settings:

  • Anti-phishing code. You create a personalised 6–8 character code that appears in legitimate Binance communications, helping you tell genuine messages apart from phishing attempts. If a message claims to be from Binance but does not carry your code, treat it as suspicious.
  • Withdrawal address whitelist. Withdrawals are limited to addresses you have pre-approved. Adding a new address triggers email verification and a safety delay, so even a fully compromised login cannot simply drain funds to an unknown wallet.
  • Passkeys. Sign in with your device’s fingerprint or face instead of a password. The credential stays on your device and resists phishing and SIM-swap, because there is no code or password to intercept.

OKX provides the same kind of layered controls — authenticator-app 2FA, an anti-phishing code and withdrawal whitelisting — in its own security centre. The names differ slightly, but the shape is the same: switch off SMS, switch on an authenticator, and restrict where funds can go.

Step 5: Put a lock on your phone number itself

Your exchange and email are now hardened, but the phone number is still yours in name only if a criminal can move it. Close that route at the network:

  • Ask your mobile network what SIM-swap protection is available on your account. Where supported, add an account PIN or an extra authentication requirement before any SIM replacement, and keep your current contact details on file so you can react quickly.
  • Vodacom: a SIM swap has a two-hour waiting period and an SMS is sent to the old SIM. If you did not request the swap, call 082 135 to stop it before the window closes.

The common thread: phone your provider and make sure there is a barrier — a PIN, a password or at least a waiting period — between an attacker and a SIM change on your number. It is a five-minute call that removes the most direct path an attacker has.

Separately, register for a free SAFPS Protective Registration. The Southern African Fraud Prevention Service flags your identity with member banks and credit providers so they take extra care before approving anything in your name. You get a reference number within about 48 hours, and it is genuinely free. Reach SAFPS on 011 867 2234 or at safps.org.za. One caution: do not hand over identity documents or personal details in response to unsolicited WhatsApp messages claiming to represent SAFPS — start from the official SAFPS website or published contact numbers instead.

Step 6: Limit how much a breach can cost you

Even a well-locked account can be compromised — through a device left logged in, a compromised app, or an inside job you never saw. The last line of defence is to keep the amount at risk small: hold only the crypto you are actively trading on an exchange, and move long-term holdings to self-custody, where the private key — not a phone number or a support desk — controls the funds. How to weigh self-custody against keeping funds on an exchange is covered in our crypto wallets guide.

Red flags: you may already be swapped

A SIM swap usually announces itself before the damage is done. If you see these together, act immediately rather than waiting:

  • Sudden loss of signal or “SOS only” while people around you still have coverage.
  • You cannot make or receive calls or SMS, and messages stop arriving.
  • Unexpected password-reset emails, login alerts, or 2FA prompts you did not trigger.
  • A message from your network about a SIM change you did not request.

If a swap has already happened

Speed matters more than completeness. In order:

  1. Call your network immediately — from another phone — and ask them to reverse or suspend the SIM swap.
  2. Contact your exchange and ask to freeze the account or suspend withdrawals while you sort it out.
  3. Retake your email first from a device still logged in: change the password, remove the phone number as a recovery method, and add an authenticator app.
  4. Move every money account off SMS 2FA onto an authenticator, and change the passwords.
  5. Report it: open a case with SAPS and ask SAFPS about a Victim of Impersonation letter, which helps with banks and credit providers afterwards.

Recognising the social tricks that lead to a takeover in the first place is a separate question — the red flags and reporting channels are in our scam prevention guide.

The checklist

  • ☐ Authenticator app on your primary email, SMS recovery removed.
  • ☐ Authenticator app on every exchange, SMS 2FA switched off, backup key on paper.
  • ☐ Dedicated crypto email with no forwarding rules you did not create.
  • ☐ Password manager with 2FA, unique password per account.
  • ☐ Anti-phishing code, withdrawal whitelist and passkeys enabled on your exchange.
  • ☐ SIM swap protection on your network, SAFPS Protective Registration done.
  • ☐ Long-term holdings moved off the exchange to self-custody.
