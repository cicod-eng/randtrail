SARS and Crypto in South Africa

Last updated: 12 September 2026

SARS treats crypto as an asset, not money — and it is watching more closely than ever. Buying and holding is generally not taxed, but the moment you dispose of crypto (sell, swap or spend it), a gain can trigger either income tax at up to 45% or capital gains tax, which for individuals is 40% included and effectively tops out near 18%. Since 1 March 2026, South Africa’s Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF) has required relevant crypto service providers to collect reportable customer and transaction information for submission to SARS — and SARS has said that more than 5.8 million South Africans hold crypto assets, now brought into its compliance programmes.

This page explains how SARS sees crypto, what it can now find out about you, and what to do if you are behind on declaring. It is general information, not tax advice — if your history is messy or your holdings are material, a South African tax practitioner is worth the fee.

Quick answer

  • SARS classifies crypto as an intangible asset, not currency. Normal income tax and capital gains tax rules apply; there is no separate “crypto tax” law.
  • Holding is generally not taxable. Selling, swapping, spending, mining and staking can be.
  • Two buckets. Active, short-term trading looks like income (marginal rates 18–45%); long-term investing looks like a capital gain (40% included, effectively 7.2% to 18%).
  • CARF started 1 March 2026. Relevant reporting crypto-asset service providers must collect and report prescribed transaction data; the first reporting period ends 28 February 2027.
  • SARS is stepping up compliance. It says more than 5.8 million South Africans hold crypto, now brought into its compliance programmes.
  • Behind on declaring? The Voluntary Disclosure Programme is a route to regularise previously undisclosed tax defaults before SARS notifies you of an audit.

How SARS classifies crypto

SARS treats crypto as an asset of an intangible nature, not as a currency or foreign exchange. There is no standalone crypto tax regime — the ordinary income tax and capital gains tax rules that apply to any other asset apply here too. The onus is on you to declare crypto-related taxable income in the tax year it is received or accrued.

In July 2026 SARS published a draft guide to the taxation of crypto assets (open for public comment until 31 August 2026), confirming that crypto is treated as intangible property under the existing rules. A draft is SARS’s interpretive position, not the final word, but it is the clearest signal yet of how SARS intends to apply the law.

What SARS can now see: CARF reporting

The biggest change for South African crypto holders is not a new tax — it is visibility. CARF, the OECD’s Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework, took effect in South Africa on 1 March 2026. Under it, reporting crypto asset service providers — exchanges, brokers, dealers, custody providers and trading platforms with a South African nexus — must collect and report customer identity data and transaction data to SARS.

That reporting covers crypto-to-crypto swaps, fiat-to-crypto purchases, crypto-to-fiat disposals and wallet transfers. In practice, CARF gives SARS much more third-party data to compare with what taxpayers declare — which can include data obtained from local providers and, through international exchange arrangements, participating foreign jurisdictions.

Two points matter for you. First, you do not report under CARF yourself — you keep declaring your gains on your normal income tax return (ITR12). CARF’s job is to give SARS third-party data to check against what you declare. Second, CARF is a tax-reporting obligation. It is separate from the licensing side of regulation, where the FSCA authorises crypto asset service providers — see our FSCA & CASP guide for that distinction.

SARS is increasing crypto compliance activity

SARS has said that more than 5.8 million South Africans hold crypto assets and has brought crypto into its compliance programmes. Tax practitioners have separately reported the creation of a dedicated Crypto Revenue Augmentation Unit in 2026 to strengthen crypto-related audit and enforcement work. The practical implication is that undeclared crypto activity is becoming easier for SARS to identify through third-party data matching.

The tax bill in brief: income vs capital

How much you owe turns on one question SARS asks: were you trading or investing? Active, short-term trading looks like income, taxed at your marginal rate (18–45%); long-term holding for appreciation looks like a capital gain, with only 40% of the net gain included. There is no fixed holding period — South Africa has no bright-line rule, so SARS looks at the facts. The full breakdown, worked examples and how to file are in our crypto tax guide.

For individuals. The 40% inclusion rate and the 18% maximum effective rate are set out in SARS’s capital gains tax rates, and the R50,000 annual exclusion (up from R40,000) in its annual exclusion rules. Companies and trusts are generally 80% included rather than 40%.

How to declare

You declare crypto gains in your normal annual return — the ITR12 — for the tax year the disposal happened. If you have material crypto income, provisional tax may also apply, so you are paying during the year rather than in a single lump at filing time. Keep records that let you reconstruct every disposal: the date, the asset, the ZAR value at the time, the amount, fees and who the counterparty was. Exchange statements and wallet history are the starting point — if you need to pull yours together, see our Binance review and OKX review for how each platform surfaces your transaction history.

Missed a year? The Voluntary Disclosure Programme

If you have crypto gains from earlier years that you never declared, SARS’s Voluntary Disclosure Programme (VDP) is the main route to regularise those tax defaults. Coming forward voluntarily can bring relief from understatement penalties and, in the right circumstances, protection from criminal prosecution for the disclosed default. Interest relief is a separate request and is not automatic.

The catch that trips people up: as a general rule, apply before SARS notifies you of an audit or criminal investigation relating to the default. Once such a notice has been issued, VDP eligibility is usually lost, subject to limited statutory exceptions. Applications go in on the VDP01 form via eFiling or a SARS branch appointment, and SARS lists a VDP line on 0800 864 613. Given the stakes, most people with a real history to regularise use a tax practitioner to prepare the disclosure rather than doing it cold.

Mistakes that get South Africans caught out

  • Assuming an offshore platform means SARS can’t see you. CARF’s international exchange is built to hand that data back across borders.
  • Assuming “no rand, no tax.” A coin-to-coin swap is a disposal even when no ZAR moved.
  • Treating the bank withdrawal as the taxable event. The disposal that created the rand — the sale — is what matters, not moving the rand out.
  • Assuming a long hold makes it capital. There is no automatic holding-period rule; SARS looks at the whole picture.
  • Waiting to declare until SARS asks. By then the VDP option is usually gone, and penalties come on top of the tax.
