Crypto Transfers & Networks in South Africa

Last updated: 12 September 2026

The single rule that governs every crypto transfer is: the network must match on both sides. The same asset — USDT, for example — exists on several different blockchains (TRC-20, ERC-20, BEP-20, Solana and more), and choosing the wrong network can make a deposit fail to appear and may result in permanent loss — especially when the receiving platform does not support that network. Recovery is sometimes possible, but it should never be assumed. Everything else in this guide is a way of making sure that one rule is never broken.

Networks differ in cost and speed, and those numbers change over time. But the cheapest network is only correct if the receiving wallet or exchange supports that same network. So the receiving side decides the network — not the fee.

Quick answer

  • Match the network — the network you select when sending must equal the one the recipient selected. A mismatch can be difficult or impossible to recover, depending on the receiving wallet or exchange.
  • Check four things before every transfer: the asset, the network, the full address, and any memo/tag.
  • Memo/tag — required for some assets (XRP, XLM, ATOM, EOS, BNB and others); leaving it out or getting it wrong stops the deposit being credited.
  • Fees — a network fee plus, on an exchange, a withdrawal fee. The fee shown at the time of sending is what counts.
  • Wrong network is the serious one — a missing or wrong memo may be recoverable on some exchanges but is not guaranteed, while a wrong-network transfer is often harder to recover. Prevent it, do not plan to fix it.

What a “network” actually is

A coin’s name is not enough to move it. USDT is issued on several independent blockchains, and each one has its own addresses and its own rules. Sending USDT “on TRC-20” is a completely different transaction from USDT “on ERC-20”, even though the token looks identical. The four you will meet most often as a South African user:

This is the durable picture, not a fee guide. Costs and speeds change, and not every exchange supports every network for every asset.

Notice the trap in that table: ERC-20 and BEP-20 addresses both start with “0x”. The address format alone cannot tell you which chain you are on, so you must confirm the network explicitly — the “0x” addresses are where most wrong-network mistakes happen.

The two checks that prevent most losses

  1. Confirm the network name, not just the coin name. On the sending side, choose the same network the recipient told you to use. A receiving exchange shows a specific network list on its deposit screen — pick from that list, not from habit.
  2. Copy the address and check the first and last several characters before sending. Do not type a long crypto address manually, and make sure the pasted address has not changed unexpectedly.

Memo / tag: the second address

Some blockchains give many users one shared deposit address, and use a memo (also called a tag, destination tag or note) to work out whose funds are whose. Assets that commonly require a memo include XRP, XLM, ATOM, EOS and BNB (and a longer list Binance maintains) — but do not memorise a list of coins that “need a memo”. The deposit screen is authoritative: if the receiving platform shows a tag/memo/comment field, copy it exactly, because leaving it out can stop the deposit from being credited.

  • No memo shown = no memo needed. Only fill it in when the deposit screen displays one.
  • Missing or wrong memo — the funds usually reach the right address on the right network, but the exchange cannot credit them automatically. Recovery may be available on some exchanges but is not guaranteed: Binance has a self-service retrieval for wrong or missing tag/memo deposits, while OKX warns that a missing tag can lead to permanent loss.
  • Memo belonging to another user — the deposit may be credited to that user, and the exchange generally cannot help. Get the memo right the first time.

On OKX, the deposit screen shows the address and tag together (with a warning for assets that need one) — see OKX’s address and tag guide.

How to deposit crypto

  1. Open Deposit and choose the coin (e.g. USDT).
  2. Select the network from the list the app shows. Note that a platform may not support every network for a given asset — OKX’s deposit guide, for instance, has flagged that BSC-based USDT deposits are not supported — so choose from what is actually listed.
  3. Copy the address, and the memo/tag if one is shown.
  4. On the sending side, pick the same network and paste both fields, then send a small test amount first for a first-time or large transfer.

How to withdraw crypto

  1. Open Withdraw, choose the coin and enter the destination address from the receiving wallet or exchange.
  2. Select the network the receiving side supports — check the receiving side first, because it is what decides the network.
  3. Enter any memo/tag the destination requires.
  4. Review the fee and the amount you will receive, then confirm (usually with 2FA and a code sent to your email or authenticator).

For the full Binance withdrawal flow, including the self-custody option, see How to withdraw from Binance.

What a transfer actually costs

Two separate charges can apply, and people often confuse them:

  • On-chain network cost — when you send from a self-custody wallet, the blockchain requires a network fee paid in the chain’s native token (TRX on Tron, ETH on Ethereum, BNB on BNB Smart Chain, SOL on Solana). It varies by network and by how busy the network is — Ethereum fees in particular rise and fall sharply with congestion, while others tend to be more stable. In a self-custody wallet you need a small balance of that native token to cover the fee, which surprises new users.
  • Exchange withdrawal fee — when withdrawing from a centralised exchange, the platform sets the fee shown to you for that asset and network. It may not equal the blockchain fee exactly, and it can change without notice, so check the figure on the withdrawal screen rather than relying on an old number.

What happens if you get it wrong

The consequences are not all equal, and knowing the difference tells you whether to panic or just file a ticket:

  • Wrong network (to an exchange) — the most serious. The deposit usually cannot be credited automatically, and recovery depends on the receiving platform and the networks involved. Contact the receiving exchange’s support immediately with the TxID; some will attempt a manual recovery for a fee, but treat this as a long shot, not a plan. Binance’s unsuccessful withdrawal FAQ covers what it can and cannot assist with, and its Academy guide explains the cases where a wrong-network transfer might be recoverable.
  • Wrong network (to a wallet you control) — recovery may be possible, particularly between compatible EVM networks where you control the private key. Do not assume this works across unrelated blockchains.
  • Missing or wrong memo — the funds reached the right address and network, so this is usually recoverable through the platform’s retrieval process (see the memo section above).
  • Wrong address (a valid but incorrect one) — usually permanent. No one can force a return from a stranger’s address.
  • Invalid address — most wallets reject it before sending, which is your safety net.

Anyone who contacts you offering “guaranteed recovery” or asking for your seed phrase or an upfront payment is a scammer. See scam prevention for crypto for the full list.

The pre-send checklist

  • The asset is correct.
  • The network matches on both sides.
  • The address is pasted (not typed) and I have checked the first and last few characters.
  • The memo/tag is filled in if (and only if) the destination requires it.
  • I have checked the fee and the amount I will receive.
  • For a large or first-time transfer, I have sent a small test amount first.

Whitelisting a withdrawal address in your account security settings adds one more layer: a new address cannot receive withdrawals without a confirmation step, which blocks the most common attack of a hacked account draining funds to an attacker’s address.

Moving USDT between platforms?

Whether you are funding a wallet, moving USDT to P2P or cashing out, the same rule applies — match the network and check the fee before you send. Both Binance and OKX let you hold and move USDT across multiple networks.
