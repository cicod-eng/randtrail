How to Withdraw from Binance in South Africa

Last updated: 12 September 2026

Getting rand out of Binance and into your South African bank account usually comes down to three routes: a direct ZAR withdrawal through the payment methods currently shown in your account, selling on P2P, or moving the crypto to a licensed local exchange such as VALR or Luno and cashing out there. The right route depends on which withdrawal methods your account displays, the fee quoted at the time and how quickly you need the money.

One thing to settle early: moving ZAR from the exchange to your bank account is generally not a separate disposal event. The tax consequence usually arises earlier, when you sell, swap, spend or otherwise dispose of the crypto. We come back to the tax part at the end.

Quick answer

  • Direct ZAR withdrawal — Binance currently documents Absa Pay as a ZAR withdrawal route, with other payment methods possibly appearing depending on your account, bank and provider availability.
  • P2P — sell your crypto to a buyer who pays rand into your bank account. Often faster, but you are dealing with another person, so stay inside the platform’s escrow.
  • Crypto to a local exchange — send the asset to VALR or Luno, sell it for rand there and withdraw. A licensed South African platform can help if your bank is cautious about crypto-sourced transfers.
  • Name matching — your Binance account name and your bank account holder name must match exactly, or the withdrawal can be rejected.
  • Tax — moving ZAR from the exchange to your bank account is generally not a separate disposal event. The tax consequence usually arises earlier, when you sell, swap, spend or otherwise dispose of the crypto.

Route 1 — Direct ZAR withdrawal to your bank

Binance currently documents Absa Pay as a ZAR withdrawal route, with step-by-step guidance in its help centre. Other withdrawal methods may appear depending on your account, bank and current provider availability. The exact methods, limits and fees shown vary by account and change over time, so always start from what your own account displays.

Step 1: Finish identity verification

Complete full verification with a South African ID document or passport, plus any proof-of-address step the platform asks for. Verification affects which withdrawal methods and limits you see.

Step 2: Open Withdraw and choose ZAR

Go to the withdraw screen, select fiat (ZAR), then choose the payment method available to you — for example Absa Pay or a bank transfer.

Step 3: Add your bank details

Enter the bank account holder name, account number and branch code. The holder name must match your Binance account name exactly, or the withdrawal can be rejected.

Step 4: Enter the amount and check the fee

Enter the amount and review the fee and the final amount shown before you confirm. Fees and limits vary, so the number on screen is the one that counts.

Step 5: Confirm and wait for processing

Confirm with two-factor authentication. Bank payouts can take from within a day up to a few business days, depending on the method and your bank.

For the full picture of Binance’s fees, ZAR funding and safety in South Africa, see our Binance South Africa review.

Route 2 — Sell on P2P for ZAR

P2P lets you sell crypto to a buyer who pays rand directly into your bank account. It can be faster than a bank transfer, but you are trading with another person, so the price, payment method and speed depend on the buyer — and the platform’s escrow is your main protection. Binance’s official guide explains how P2P and its escrow work.

Step 1: Set up P2P

Complete verification and add the bank account that will receive the buyer’s payment.

Step 2: Find a buy offer

Look for buyers of your crypto and compare their price, completion rate and limits. A price that looks much better than the market is a reason to be cautious, not to rush.

Step 3: Wait for the buyer’s payment

Once the order starts, Binance reserves the crypto in escrow while the buyer makes payment to your selected bank account.

Step 4: Check your bank account, then release

Confirm that the rand has actually arrived in your bank account before tapping Payment received or releasing the crypto. Do not rely on screenshots or SMS notifications alone. Never trade outside the platform’s P2P process. For the scams to watch for, read our P2P guide for South Africa.

Route 3 — Move crypto to VALR or Luno and withdraw

If your bank is cautious about crypto-sourced transfers, or you simply prefer to cash out through a licensed South African platform, you can send the crypto to VALR or Luno, sell it for rand there and withdraw to your bank. This is a two-step route, so you pay a network fee on the transfer and a sell fee on the local exchange.

Step 1: Set up the local exchange

Create and verify an account, then get a deposit address for the asset you are sending.

Step 2: Check the network matches

The sending and receiving side must use the same network — sending USDT on the wrong network can make the funds unrecoverable. For how to pick the right network, read Transfers & networks.

Step 3: Send and sell

Send the crypto, wait for it to arrive, then sell it for rand on the local exchange.

Step 4: Withdraw rand to your bank

Withdraw the rand to your bank account, checking the local exchange’s withdrawal fee and time first.

What it really costs

  • Withdrawal — the fee and any processing charge vary by method and account. Check the number shown before you confirm.
  • Network — if you move crypto between exchanges, the network fee depends on the network you choose and how busy it is.
  • Selling — the spread or trading fee when you convert crypto to rand.
  • P2P — fees can vary by market and order type. Even where no separate fee is shown, compare the buyer’s quoted ZAR price with the wider market, because the economic cost may sit in the quote.

If you are still on the buying side, the same logic applies in reverse — see how to buy USDT with ZAR for the cost of getting rand in.

Why a withdrawal gets stuck or rejected

  • Name mismatch — a mismatch between your verified account name and bank details can cause a withdrawal to fail or require review.
  • Incomplete verification — the withdrawal method you want is not available until verification is finished.
  • Bank flags the transfer — some South African banks are more cautious about crypto-sourced payments than others. A licensed local exchange can sometimes be a smoother route.
  • Wrong network on a crypto transfer — sending to an address on the wrong network can mean the funds are not recoverable.
  • Withdrawal under review — large or unusual withdrawals can be held for additional checks.

What SARS actually taxes

Moving ZAR from the exchange to your bank account is generally not a separate disposal event. The tax question is what happened before that — the gain on the asset when you sold, swapped, spent or otherwise disposed of it. SARS treats crypto assets under normal tax principles — a gain can be revenue in nature (taxed as income, for frequent or short-term trading) or capital in nature (taxed under capital gains rules, for longer-term investment). The classification depends on the facts, not on a simple label.

Keep records of the date, ZAR value, fees and proceeds of every disposal. South Africa’s Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF) took effect on 1 March 2026, and relevant service providers must report certain user and transaction information to SARS — individual users still declare their own gains in the normal return. See SARS’s crypto assets & tax page and our Crypto tax in South Africa guide for what to track.
