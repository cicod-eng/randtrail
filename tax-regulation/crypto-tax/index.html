Crypto Tax in South Africa

Last updated: 12 September 2026

If you hold crypto in South Africa, SARS expects you to declare the gains when you dispose of it — and since 1 March 2026, South Africa’s CARF reporting framework has been in force, increasing the amount of crypto transaction data that relevant service providers must collect and report to SARS. Buying and holding is not a taxable event, but selling, swapping, spending, mining and staking generally are. Whether a gain is taxed as income (up to 45%) or as a capital gain (effectively up to about 18% for individuals) depends on how you behave, not on a label you choose.

This guide explains what triggers tax, how the two tax buckets work, the numbers to know, and how to declare without getting it wrong. It is general information, not tax advice — if your holdings are material or your history is messy, a South African tax practitioner is worth the fee.

Quick answer

  • Buying and holding by itself is generally not taxable. Tax can arise when you dispose of crypto, and some activities such as mining or staking can create taxable income when rewards are received.
  • Selling, swapping, spending, mining and staking can all trigger tax. Swapping one coin for another is a disposal even if no rand changes hands.
  • Income vs capital depends on the facts. Active, short-term trading looks like income (up to 45%); long-term holding looks like a capital gain (effectively up to about 18% for individuals).
  • There is no fixed holding period. South Africa has no bright-line rule that makes a gain “capital” after a set time.
  • CARF started 1 March 2026. Platforms now report certain transaction data to SARS, but you still declare your own gains in your normal return.

What triggers tax — and what doesn’t

SARS treats crypto as an asset of an intangible nature, not as currency. The taxable moment is the disposal — when you give up an asset and a gain or loss is realised. Here is how common actions map to that rule.

Income vs capital: the decision that sets your rate

This is where most of the money is decided, and it is also where most guides go vague. SARS does not give you a checkbox — it asks what you were actually doing. The distinction matters because income is taxed at 100% of the gain up to 45%, while a capital gain is only 40% included.

Signals that point towards income (active trading):

  • You trade frequently, with short holding periods and repeat buy-sell cycles.
  • You treat crypto as a source of regular profit, or you have a systematic strategy.
  • You trade with borrowed money or leverage.

Signals that point towards capital (long-term investment):

  • You buy and hold for a long time, with few disposals.
  • Your aim is long-term appreciation, not short-term profit-taking.

Two things that catch people out. First, there is no fixed holding period — holding for years does not automatically make a gain capital in nature, and trading once does not automatically make it income. Second, you can be both at once: a long-term stack alongside an active trading pot is legitimate, but you need to be able to show which is which when SARS asks. See our Tax & Regulation guides for the broader SARS and FSCA picture.

The numbers: income tax vs capital gains

For individuals, the two routes look like this:

  • Income (revenue) — the full gain is added to your taxable income and taxed at your marginal rate, which runs from 18% up to 45% across the brackets. There is no inclusion discount and no annual exclusion.
  • Capital gain — only 40% of the net gain is included in taxable income, after the annual exclusion that applies to individuals for that tax year. Check the current SARS threshold before calculating your return. The included amount is then taxed at your marginal rate, capping the effective rate at about 18% for an individual in the top bracket.

The inclusion rate differs by entity: individuals are 40%, while companies and trusts are generally 80% included (which lands companies around 21.6% effective and trusts around 36% effective at current top rates). Most people reading this are individuals, so the 40% / ~18% pair is the one that matters.

A worked example makes the gap concrete. For illustration, using an annual exclusion of R40,000 — buy crypto for R100,000 (including fees, as base cost), sell it later for R250,000, so the gain is R150,000:

Key dates to keep in mind

  • 1 March 2026 — the Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF) took effect in South Africa.
  • 1 March 2026 to 28 February 2027 — the first CARF reporting period, which providers report on to SARS (with international exchange expected to follow around September 2027).
  • July 2026 — SARS published a draft guide to the taxation of crypto assets, confirming crypto is treated as intangible property under existing income-tax and CGT rules.
  • 2026 filing season — covers the 1 March 2025 to 28 February 2026 tax year; it opened in July 2026, with the non-provisional deadline in October 2026 and the provisional deadline in January 2027. Confirm the exact dates for your own return.

What to keep for SARS

Keep records that let you reconstruct every disposal: the date, the asset, the ZAR value at the time, the amount, fees, and who the counterparty was. Include exchange statements, wallet addresses and any notes on why a particular trade was part of an investing rather than a trading activity. Keep supporting records for at least five years after filing, and longer where SARS rules require it for your circumstances.

If you haven’t declared before

SARS has stood up a dedicated crypto team and, through CARF, is receiving transaction data from service providers — so past activity is increasingly visible to it. If you have previously undeclared crypto income or gains, SARS’s Voluntary Disclosure Programme (VDP) may provide a route to regularise your tax affairs and obtain relief from certain penalties, depending on the circumstances. Interest and other amounts may still apply.

Mistakes that get South Africans into trouble

  • Assuming “no rand, no tax” — a coin-to-coin swap is a disposal even though no ZAR moved.
  • Assuming a long hold makes it capital — there is no automatic holding-period rule.
  • Treating a withdrawal as the taxable event — moving rand to your bank is generally not the disposal; the sale that produced the rand was.
  • Mixing a trading pot and a long-term stack with no records — if you cannot show which is which, SARS may treat the whole thing as trading income.
