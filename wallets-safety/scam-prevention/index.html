Crypto Scams in South Africa

Last updated: 12 September 2026

Most crypto scams in South Africa are not technical hacks — they are social tricks aimed at people, not code. A WhatsApp or Telegram stranger promising guaranteed returns, a fake platform that shows profits it will never pay out, a “support agent” who contacts you first, or a SIM-swap that hijacks your two-factor codes. Two rules filter out almost all of it: guaranteed returns are always a scam, and anyone who contacts you first asking for money or keys is a scam. If you have already sent money, the priority is to stop paying, alert your bank and the exchange, then report it to SAPS and the FSCA.

This guide covers the scams doing the rounds in 2026, the red flags to recognise, how to check a platform before you send a rand, and exactly where to report in South Africa. It is educational information, not financial or legal advice.

Quick answer

  • South African crypto scams are overwhelmingly social engineering — WhatsApp and Telegram approaches, fake investment platforms and impersonation — not hacks of your device.
  • Two red flags catch most of them: guaranteed or exaggerated returns, and being contacted first by someone asking for money or your keys.
  • Never share your recovery phrase, password or OTP. Use an authenticator app rather than SMS 2FA — SMS codes can be stolen through SIM-swap fraud.
  • Verify before you send: check the FSCA’s authorised list, call its verification line, and confirm the company on CIPC.
  • If you have sent money: stop all further payments, alert your bank and the exchange, keep the evidence, and report to SAPS and the FSCA.
  • Watch for the second hit: “recovery” services that promise to get your money back for an upfront fee are usually the same criminals again.

The scams hitting South Africans right now

The FSCA has been publishing public warnings at a steady clip through 2026, and several follow the same playbook. In June 2026 it warned the public against Fast Profit Income, an outfit soliciting crypto and forex investments with promises of “up to 200%” returns, which the regulator confirmed is not authorised. In May 2026 it warned of Telegram groups impersonating established financial firms — Prime Investments, Fairtree Asset Management and PWM Wealth Management — using their names and branding to push Bitcoin mining and trading. Around the same time it flagged a deepfake video impersonating South African financial journalists to promote “Quantum AI”, with claimed returns of tens of thousands of rand a week from a small deposit.

The lesson from all three: the old tells — broken English, clumsy graphics — are no longer reliable. A scam can now arrive with a familiar face on screen and a polished website.

How the common scams work

Strip away the details and nearly every crypto scam is one of a handful of patterns:

  • Pig-butchering (romance or long-game scams). Someone builds a relationship over days or weeks on WhatsApp or Telegram, then introduces an “investment” with fake screenshots of gains. The friendship is the bait.
  • Fake investment platforms. You deposit, the site shows a growing balance, but withdrawals fail — until you pay a “tax”, “unlock fee” or “withdrawal fee” that never ends.
  • Impersonation. A “support agent”, “admin” or “exchange representative” contacts you first — real platforms do not message you asking for passwords, OTPs or your seed phrase.
  • Recovery scams. After you have already lost money, a “lawyer” or “recovery firm” offers to trace it for an upfront fee. This is a second scam on the same victim.

None of these require technical skill. They run on urgency, authority and greed — which is why awareness beats antivirus software here. The same applies to P2P trading, where most problems are social rather than technical: a buyer who “accidentally” overpays, or pressure to release crypto before the money clears.

SIM-swap: the South African angle

South Africa has a fraud problem most crypto guides written elsewhere miss: SIM-swap fraud. A criminal convinces your mobile network — Vodacom, MTN, Cell C or Telkom — to move your number onto a SIM they control. From there they intercept the SMS one-time PINs that protect your email, banking and exchange accounts, and reset their way in. Industry figures have reported telecom losses from SIM-swap fraud exceeding R5 billion in 2025, and it remains an active threat — with authorities warning of a rise in parts of Gauteng in July 2026.

Crypto holders are a favourite target because SMS 2FA is common and because an on-chain transfer, once made, is generally irreversible — there is no fraud desk to reverse it. The single most effective defence is to stop using SMS as your second factor for anything that holds money: switch to an authenticator app or a hardware security key, and remove your phone number as a recovery method where the platform allows. The full checklist is in our account security guide.

The red flags

One flag is suspicious; two together are a near-certainty. These are the ones that keep showing up in FSCA warnings:

  • Guaranteed or exaggerated returns — “200%”, “1000%”, “R60,000 a week”. No legitimate investment guarantees profit.
  • Approached on WhatsApp, Telegram or social media rather than a platform you chose yourself.
  • Upfront payments — “training fees”, “account activation”, “taxes” before you can withdraw.
  • Pressure to act fast — urgency is a manipulation tool, not a feature.
  • Vague product details — you cannot explain what the money actually does.
  • Requests for your seed phrase, password or OTP — no legitimate service ever needs these.
  • Celebrity or “expert” endorsements as proof, or a licence number that does not match the legal entity.

Verify before you send a rand

Before handing money to any platform or person, run it through the official checks. They take a few minutes and rule out most fraud:

  • Check the FSCA. Look up the entity on the FSCA’s authorised financial services providers, or call its toll-free verification line on 0800 110 443. Make sure the name matches exactly — scammers often impersonate a licensed firm.
  • Check CIPC. Confirm the company actually exists and is “active” on the BizPortal register, not deregistered.
  • Find the real site yourself. Type the address or search it — do not click links sent over WhatsApp or Telegram.
  • Match the licence category. An FSP number for a low-risk product does not cover complex crypto or forex advice.

Verifying a platform’s licence is a different question from whether it is authorised under South Africa’s crypto-asset framework — see our FSCA & CASP guide for how the two fit together.

If you have already sent money

Speed matters, and so does stopping the bleed. In order:

  1. Stop all further payments. Never pay an “unlock fee”, “tax” or “recovery fee” to release your money — it is how the scam continues.
  2. Contact your bank or card provider immediately and report the payment as suspected fraud.
  3. Contact the exchange you used and provide the transaction details and the receiving wallet address.
  4. Preserve evidence. Screenshots of chats, phone numbers, wallet addresses, transaction hashes and bank records — before the scammer deletes them.
  5. Open a case with SAPS and keep the case number.

Be realistic about recovery: once crypto has moved, tracing it is difficult and never guaranteed. Anyone who promises otherwise — especially for an upfront fee — is very likely a recovery scam.

Mistakes that keep the scam going

  • Paying to release your money. “Tax”, “unlock fee”, “withdrawal fee” — each payment just extends the scam.
  • Trusting a recovery service that contacts you. Real recovery does not cold-call victims.
  • Giving out an OTP or seed phrase to “verify” yourself. No legitimate platform asks for these.
  • Keeping SMS 2FA for your exchange after you know SIM-swap is a risk.
  • Moving the conversation to private chat. Keep trades on the exchange, where the escrow and audit trail live.
