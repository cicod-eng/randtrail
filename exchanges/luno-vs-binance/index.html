Luno vs Binance in South Africa

Last updated: 26 September 2026

Luno and Binance solve slightly different problems for South African crypto users.

Luno is a locally authorised platform built around a straightforward ZAR experience: bank deposits, rand withdrawals, a curated list of crypto assets and a relatively simple interface.

Binance is a much larger global platform with lower standard spot-trading fees, hundreds of crypto assets and several ways for South Africans to fund an account in rand, including linked bank payments, Capitec Pay, Absa Pay, bank transfer, card and Apple Pay, depending on account eligibility and the method currently shown in the app.

The practical question is therefore not simply “which exchange is better?” It is:

Do you want a simpler locally authorised ZAR platform, or a larger exchange with lower trading fees and a wider range of markets?

Two things matter before comparing headline numbers.

First, the way you buy matters as much as the advertised trading fee. Buying from an instant-buy screen can cost substantially more than placing an order on an exchange order book.

Second, Luno and Binance do not sit in exactly the same regulatory position in South Africa. That distinction is worth understanding rather than reducing it to a simple “licensed vs unlicensed” label.

Quick answer

  • Luno — straightforward local ZAR deposits and withdrawals, South African FSP authorisation and a smaller curated crypto list.
  • Binance — standard spot fees of 0.10% maker / 0.10% taker for regular users before applicable discounts, plus a much broader selection of crypto markets.
  • Luno instant buy/sell — currently carries a 2% transaction cost when buying or selling from the Portfolio screen. This is separate from Luno Exchange fees.
  • Luno Exchange — for a new or low-volume user trading most crypto/ZAR pairs, the current Tier 1 rate is 0.40% maker / 0.60% taker. Stablecoin/ZAR pairs have a different fee schedule.
  • ZAR funding — both platforms support direct rand funding, but their fees and payment methods differ considerably.
  • Regulation — Luno (Pty) Ltd states that it is an authorised Financial Services Provider, FSP 53314. Binance uses different entities and regulatory arrangements depending on the product, particularly for derivatives in South Africa.

Fees: where the difference really appears

Comparing “0.10% versus 0.60%” does not tell the whole story. You first need to distinguish between:

  1. depositing rand;
  2. buying through an instant-buy interface;
  3. trading on an order book.

These can have completely different costs.

Luno fees

For South African accounts, Luno currently lists these deposit fees:

Fees are the figures Luno currently lists for South African accounts and can change. Normal ZAR withdrawals are currently listed as free, while express withdrawals carry a fee.

There is then a second cost when you actually buy crypto. If you use Luno’s Portfolio instant-buy or instant-sell interface, the current transaction cost is 2%. That is very different from using the Luno Exchange. For most crypto/ZAR pairs, a Tier 1 user with less than R20,000 in 30-day trading volume currently pays 0.40% maker and 0.60% taker. USDT/ZAR and USDC/ZAR use a separate fee schedule, and Luno is also running temporary fee arrangements on certain ZAR stablecoin markets, so those pairs should be checked separately rather than assuming the BTC/ZAR rate applies to everything.

One important correction to older guides: direct card buying is currently unavailable on Luno in South Africa, according to Luno’s current help page.

Binance fees

For a regular Binance user, the published standard spot fee is currently 0.10% maker and 0.10% taker. Binance also publishes lower rates for qualifying VIP users and a fee discount where eligible users pay trading fees with BNB. That makes the order-book fee considerably lower than Luno’s entry-level rate on most ZAR crypto pairs.

But do not interpret this as “buying on Binance always costs 0.10%.” Your total cost can also include the ZAR deposit or payment fee, the quoted exchange rate, the trading fee, and withdrawal or blockchain-network fees if you later move the asset. The correct number is therefore the total ZAR paid compared with the crypto actually received, not just the fee printed on the trading page.

A simple cost example

Imagine you want to put R10,000 into Bitcoin. On Luno, buying directly from the Portfolio screen at the current 2% transaction cost would mean a transaction cost equivalent to about R200, before considering movements in the quoted crypto price. Using the Luno Exchange as a Tier 1 taker at 0.60% would instead mean approximately R60 in trading fees. A standard 0.10% Binance spot trade would be approximately R10 in trading fees — but you would still need to include whatever cost applies to the ZAR funding method you used.

That is why comparing instant buy versus exchange trading matters more than simply comparing the names Luno and Binance.

ZAR deposits

Luno

Luno’s rand funding setup is designed around South African payment methods. The current official fee page lists normal EFT, instant deposit, Absa Pay, Capitec Pay and Apple Pay. A normal EFT is currently listed as free, while the faster payment methods carry their own charges. For someone who simply wants to transfer rand into an exchange account and then buy BTC, ETH or USDT, the process is relatively easy to understand.

Binance

Binance now has several documented ZAR funding routes for South African users. Its support material currently covers linked bank payments, bank transfer, Capitec Pay, Absa Pay, card and Apple Pay. Binance introduced Capitec Pay as a direct ZAR deposit route, with payment approval taking place inside the Capitec app — read how the Capitec Pay and Binance partnership works. Binance’s July 2026 documentation also confirms Absa Pay deposits, while a September 2026 support listing includes a dedicated ZAR bank-transfer deposit guide. Binance’s current ZAR card/Apple Pay guide states that Google Pay is not supported for that particular ZAR deposit flow.

The important point is that availability and fees should be checked on the final deposit screen. Binance frequently runs temporary ZAR promotions, so an old screenshot showing “zero fees” should not be treated as a permanent fee schedule — its January 2026 Capitec Pay zero-fee offer was explicitly time-limited. For the bank-by-bank route, see How to deposit ZAR on Binance.

ZAR withdrawals

Luno offers direct ZAR withdrawal to a South African bank account. Its current fee schedule lists a normal withdrawal as free and an express withdrawal at R20 for participating real-time-clearing banks, with a higher fee for certain non-RTC banks. Binance also documents ZAR withdrawals to linked bank accounts, and in July 2026 added an official Absa Pay withdrawal flow.

Do not assume that every deposit method also works identically for withdrawal. Check the withdrawal tab in your own account before deciding how you intend to cash out. For the wider picture, read ZAR deposits in South Africa.

Coins and markets

This distinction is smaller than some older Luno comparisons suggest. Luno is still the more curated platform, but describing it as supporting only a tiny handful of assets is now outdated. Its current South African list includes assets such as BTC, ETH, SOL, XRP, ADA, AVAX, LINK, USDT, USDC, PAXG and numerous additional tokens, and its South African exchange provides direct ZAR markets for a range of major assets.

Binance nevertheless offers a substantially broader market catalogue and more trading products. That difference becomes important if you want smaller altcoins, a larger number of crypto-to-crypto pairs, trading bots, margin products, or futures and other derivatives where available. For someone buying only BTC, ETH or USDT, “number of coins” may make very little practical difference.

Luno’s regulatory position in South Africa

Luno’s position is comparatively straightforward. Luno (Pty) Ltd states that it is an authorised Financial Services Provider under South Africa’s FAIS framework, with FSP number 53314. That means it is reasonable to describe Luno as a locally authorised FSP.

It is not reasonable to translate that into “your crypto cannot be lost” or “Luno is guaranteed safe.” Regulatory authorisation does not remove crypto price risk, cybersecurity risk, user error, asset-specific risk or liquidity risk. Those are separate questions.

Binance’s regulatory position is more product-specific

Binance needs more careful wording. For South African derivatives, Binance’s current South African pages state that Nest Exchange Limited and Binance Bahrain BSC offer certain derivative products as juristic representatives of FiveWest OTC Desk (Pty) Ltd, which is an authorised South African financial services provider, FSP 51619. Binance previously announced the same juristic-representative structure for South African derivatives and in 2024 moved affected users to a Binance Bahrain arrangement for those products.

That is more precise than saying either “Binance is FSCA licensed” or “Binance is unlicensed in South Africa.” Both statements can hide the fact that the relevant entity and regulatory arrangement depend on the service you are using. If local regulatory status is important to you, check the legal entity named in the terms for the specific Binance product rather than assuming every Binance service sits under one South African licence. Tax: the exchange does not remove your SARS obligations

Using Luno instead of Binance does not make crypto gains exempt from South African tax. SARS states that the tax treatment depends on the facts and circumstances — crypto gains may be treated as ordinary revenue or as capital gains depending on the nature of the activity.

South Africa also implemented the Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF) from 1 March 2026. Under CARF, relevant crypto-asset service providers must collect and report specified customer and transaction information to SARS, and the first reporting period runs from 1 March 2026 to 28 February 2027. CARF does not create a new tax on crypto — it is a reporting framework — and individual taxpayers must still declare taxable crypto activity through the normal tax system. See Crypto tax in South Africa.

Whichever exchange you use, keep records of the acquisition date, ZAR cost, disposal date, ZAR proceeds, trading fees, deposit and withdrawal costs, crypto-to-crypto swaps, and transfers between your own wallets or exchanges.

Common mistakes when comparing Luno and Binance

1. Comparing Binance spot fees with Luno instant buy

This compares two different products. Luno Portfolio instant buying currently costs 2%, while Luno Exchange has a separate maker/taker fee schedule. Compare order book with order book, or instant buy with instant buy.

2. Assuming Luno card buying is currently available

It is not. Luno’s current South Africa card guide explicitly says that instant card buying is temporarily unavailable.

3. Using an old Binance ZAR guide

Binance’s South African payment setup changed materially during 2026. Capitec Pay and Absa Pay are now documented routes, and Binance published a new ZAR bank-transfer deposit guide in September 2026. Check the current app rather than assuming a 2024 or 2025 guide is still accurate.

4. Treating a temporary promotion as the normal fee

Binance regularly runs ZAR deposit promotions. A “zero fee” banner may apply only to a specific payment method, first transaction, eligibility group or campaign period.

5. Assuming “licensed” means risk-free

An FSP authorisation tells you something important about regulatory status. It does not guarantee your investment value, eliminate crypto volatility or protect you from every operational risk.

6. Ignoring the account name

When using bank funding routes, use an account in your own name and follow the exchange’s payment instructions. Binance’s linked-bank documentation, for example, requires the bank-account holder name to match the Binance account holder.

7. Choosing by coin count when you only buy Bitcoin

If you only buy BTC or ETH, hundreds of additional listings may add no practical value. Your real comparison may simply be: how much ZAR do I pay → how much BTC do I receive → how easily can I withdraw it later?

Final takeaway

Luno and Binance are different enough that a single headline score is not very useful. Luno gives South Africans a relatively straightforward local ZAR experience and operates locally as authorised FSP 53314; its convenience comes with an important fee distinction — instant Portfolio buys and sells currently cost 2%, while Exchange orders use a lower maker/taker structure. Binance offers much broader markets and a standard 0.10% / 0.10% spot fee for regular users, and South African users now have several documented ZAR routes including Capitec Pay, Absa Pay and bank transfer, although fees and availability can vary.

So instead of asking only “Luno or Binance?”, compare the exact route you will use: ZAR deposit → buy method → trading fee → withdrawal route → regulatory entity. That gives you a much more useful answer than comparing one advertised fee.
